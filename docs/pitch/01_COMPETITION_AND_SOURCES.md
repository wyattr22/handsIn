## Optional challenge is separate

At most one challenge per team. Entry requires the challenge selection and a publicly accessible video link by noon. Video hard cap is two minutes; aim around 1:50. This is separate from both the four-minute live pitch and a roughly twenty-second failure fallback.

- OpenAI challenge: use Codex to build something that helps a specific person or community.
- Startup Launch Award: identify the customer and test genuine demand with evidence.
- Best Use of AI: demonstrate a product that depends on AI; ordinary AI-assisted coding alone is insufficient.

Shortlists are listed by 3 PM; challenge round begins at 3:15 PM. OpenAI's shortlisted teams pitch on Zoom. If selected for both, challenge pitch precedes finals. No entry, shortlist, or qualification is established for HandsIn.

## Verified scoring tiers

The same 100-point rubric applies in rooms and finals. The guide provides low/middle/top descriptions and permits intermediate scores.

| Criterion | Allowed scores | Low | Middle | Top |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Execution and design | 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 | Nothing runs live or only slides; difficult to use | Core flow runs with gaps; usable with help; rough | Runs live without faking; easy on first contact; looks like a real product |
| Real problem, real user | 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 | Vague everyone audience; no evidence of demand | Clear problem and conversations with likely users | Specific painful problem and proof real people used it during the weekend |
| Path to customers | 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 | No model or viral hopes | Plausible untested model, vague acquisition | Clear payer, price and switching reason; specific people/groups/places for first users |
| Pitch and demo | 3, 6, 9, 12, 15 | Unclear product/user; wandering or broken demo; evasive answers | Clear product and user; main flow shown; most questions answered | Interest within 30 seconds; coherent live user story; sharp honest answers |
| Creative use of AI | 3, 6, 9, 12, 15 | Obvious code/copy use or decorative chatbot | Sensible familiar AI application | Distinctive demonstrable AI use on which product or business depends |

Code difficulty, amount of AI used, and majors are not scoring criteria. At finals, execution/design is judged from the live demo. Room tiebreaking favors Real problem first, then Execution. Scores inform judges rather than mechanically determining winners.

Real users are not an eligibility requirement, but proof raises the score. Do not manufacture use, testimonials, screenshots, interviews, revenues or research. The guide prohibits mass campus/department/club emails and says to ask the help desk before taking real money.

## Workshop guidance worth applying

The visually emphasized sections on p15 are **1, 2, 4, 8, 10**. Market, business model, traction, go-to-market and financials are de-emphasized visually, not prohibited.

- Open with a concrete customer moment or supported fact. Speaker strongly advised against show-of-hands openings.
- Connect judges to the person facing the problem; avoid a long founder autobiography.
- Address the one objection most likely to distract judges early, with concrete evidence.
- Explain why now only with a supported change or insight. Generic AI enthusiasm is not evidence.
- Demonstrate value; technical choices matter when they explain that value.
- Show why this small team can take the next realistic step after the weekend.
- Prefer one presenter, or two with a purposeful rehearsed handoff. This is advice, not a rule or assignment.
- Prepare logins, tabs, permissions, data and fallback before speaking.
- Use visual slides, readable type, one main point per slide and a memorable close.
- Prepare skeptical questions and optional backup slides.
- Do not overstate market size or invent financial projections.
- The notes' phrase about finishing the build does not establish that all features work.

No deck should simply reproduce the workshop's ten-section investor outline. Use the format that makes the rubric easiest to observe.
