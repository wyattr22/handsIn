## 2. Annotated and reviewed notes

November 15, 2026 · Tom Katona · Startup Launch Weekend

Our four-person team finished the hackathon build. The workshop covered how to present it to judges in four minutes. Use the workshop guidance to shape the pitch and the participant briefing to verify event requirements.

### Speaker's advice

#### Open strong and tell a story

- Open with a concrete customer story or a striking fact. The speaker strongly advised against opening with a question or a show of hands. A judge who thinks the question does not apply to them may tune out.
- Help the judges understand the person experiencing the problem, what their life looks like today, and what changes if the product works. Align everyone around that future.
- Establish the problem quickly and honestly. Use a credible example or evidence. Avoid exaggerating the pain or dwelling on the team's own experience unless it helps the audience understand the customer.
- Explain why now and why this has been hard to solve before. Name the change that makes it possible or worthwhile now, such as new technology, changing habits, a regulatory shift, or a new insight.
- End with what you want judges to remember.

#### Address the doubts that could derail the pitch

- Listen to the pitch as a skeptical judge. Identify the biggest objection and address it early, before it distracts from the rest of the story. Explain what the team has learned or done about it.
- The speaker used an AI-company analogy. If a customer puts their reputation on the line by trusting a machine's output, why should they trust it? Use the analogy to find the strongest objection to our own product.
- Spend time where the team can show real thinking or evidence. Keep unsupported claims and speculative details brief.
- Explain technical choices when they show how the product delivers value or why the team chose a particular approach.

#### Show the first step and why this team can take it

- The grand vision is easier to sell than the first credible step. Explain what a small team of students can do with the resources it has now and how that gets the business moving.
- Early-stage judges are betting on the people. Show the skills, lived experience, or progress that make this team credible.
- Putting the team near the end is common. Move it earlier if an unusually relevant credential or firsthand experience changes how the judges understand the pitch.
- If time permits, explain what the team will do next. Continuing after the weekend and the prize shows commitment and gives the judges a concrete plan to assess.

#### Present and demo

- The speaker recommended one presenter for a four-minute pitch. Two can work if they bring complementary strengths, such as business and product or engineering. Rehearse any handoff so it feels natural and takes little time.
- Show the product moment that proves the most important claim. Prepare logins, permissions, tabs, and any paywall access in advance so the demo can start immediately.
- Record a roughly 20-second clip of the product working as a fallback. Skip routine steps such as logging in. The speaker discussed a live component. The participant briefing below records what the event documents actually specify.
- Prepare for likely judge questions and have backup slides ready if they help answer those questions.

#### Make the deck feel like the product

- Use visuals and a single idea per slide. Dense text invites both judges and presenters to read the slide. Use readable type, high contrast, and a consistent palette of two or three colors.
- For design references, the speaker suggested [Adobe Color Explore](https://color.adobe.com/explore) for palettes, [Font Squirrel](https://www.fontsquirrel.com/) for fonts, [The Noun Project](https://thenounproject.com/) for icons, and past student Demo Day pitches for inspiration.
- The speaker described a Cal Poly team that sold comedy-based workplace harassment training. Its first pitch covered the business but felt dry. When the team returned a week later, the delivery itself showed what made the product funny and engaging.

### Ten pitch-deck sections

The workshop slides list all ten. The speaker emphasized **1, 2, 4, 8, and 10** for a four-minute pitch.

| # | Section | What to cover |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | **Problem** | Define the customer pain with clarity and evidence. |
| **2** | **Solution** | Explain what customers gain and why they would choose it. |
| 3 | Market Opportunity | Estimate market size and growth potential. |
| **4** | **Product or Technology** | Show what the product does and what makes it distinctive. |
| 5 | Business Model | Explain how the business will make money. |
| 6 | Traction | Share milestones and customer validation. |
| 7 | Go-to-Market Strategy | Explain how the team will reach early adopters. |
| **8** | **Competition** | Show the alternatives and how the product differs. |
| 9 | Financials | Give high-level projections and the assumptions behind them. |
| **10** | **Team** | Show why this team can execute. |

### Official judging rules

The participant briefing (pp. 7–9 and 13–15) and the [official judging guide](https://cpvc-ai-hackathon-guide.vercel.app/#judging) confirm these rules. The guide says event updates posted on Discord take precedence.

- **Four minutes for the pitch and live demo**, followed by **three minutes for judge questions**. The two-minute room changeover is for setup. A slide-led pitch, a product-led pitch, and a mix are all allowed. Slides are optional.
- **Run the submitted product live** on your laptop. The guide recommends identifying the user and problem in the first 30 seconds, then showing one user's path through the product.
- **Have a fallback ready.** If the demo fails, the participant briefing says to switch to a teammate's device or screenshots within about 15 seconds and keep pitching. The speaker's 20-second recording is additional backup. The optional challenge-entry video is a separate submission with a two-minute cap.

#### Scoring rubric

The same 100-point rubric applies in the judging rooms and at finals. The descriptions below summarize the guide's top scoring tier.

| Points | Criterion | What earns the top score |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 25 | Execution & design | The core flow works live as presented, feels easy to use on first contact, and looks like a finished product. |
| 25 | Real problem, real user | A defined user faces a meaningful problem. Show evidence that people actually used the product during the weekend. |
| 20 | Path to customers | Specify who pays, how much, why they would switch from their current approach, and which groups, places, or people will yield the first users. |
| 15 | Pitch & demo | Make the judges care within 30 seconds, follow one user's story in the live demo, and answer questions clearly and honestly. |
| 15 | Creative use of AI | Show an original use of AI that the product or business depends on. |

The event briefing lists user interviews or quotes, actual use, demand signals, and sourced numbers as evidence. Describe the evidence you have accurately. Keep the 20-point path to customers and the 15-point creative use of AI visible even though the workshop's five recommended sections use different labels.
